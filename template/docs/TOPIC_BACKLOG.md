# Topic backlog: 40 verified anchors

Companion to `CONTENT_FORMULA.md`. Each entry is a seed for one 60–70 s episode. Hooks, questions and lessons are drafts in the series voice, not scripts.

## Method (read first)

- **Exclusions applied.** Nothing here reuses the Zeigarnik effect or open loops, the doorway effect, the bystander or smoke-filled-room study, the CIA Gateway Process or Monroe, Cary Grant or self-perception ("fake it"), Walsch or Einstein on time, Goddard, AI rules or free will, or Palmer Luckey. Where a topic sits close to one of these, the entry says how to keep it distinct.
- **How each anchor was verified (Nov 2026).** Every anchor was checked with web search. For each one I confirmed the authors, year and venue against at least one primary or publisher record (DOI, PubMed, journal, NASA NTRS, CIA reading room, or a university-hosted copy of the paper). Where I could get the full text, I read the numbers directly from the primary source. That covers Simons & Levin (Experiment 1 and 2 counts), Freedman & Fraser (76.0% vs 16.7%), Wood et al. (43%/35%), Gollwitzer 1999 (three-fourths vs one-third), Wagner et al. (59.1% vs 22.7%), Festinger & Carlsmith (+1.35 / −0.05 / −0.45), the Simple Sabotage manual (the item wording), the AIR remote-viewing report (the conclusions), the NASA TM-108839 summary, Heuer Chapter 5, and the quotes from Seneca (Latin text), Epictetus, Marcus, Darwin, Pascal and Aristotle (Ross translation).
- **Numbers.** A number appears only if it was confirmed in the paper or in at least two consistent independent sources. Where sources disagreed, the entry gives the conservative version and says so. Figures that circulate widely but could not be confirmed are listed under **Rejected / not usable** at the end.
- **Status tags.**
  - **ROBUST:** replicated or foundational.
  - **SOLID, LIMITED:** real, but from a small sample, a single study, correlational data or an anecdote. The script should say "in one study" or "researchers found".
  - **CONTESTED:** large replications or meta-analyses failed or are mixed. The script must hedge, and the entry says how.
- **Quotes.** Each quote is traced to a primary text, with the work, section and translator given. Public-domain translations are used so the exact wording can be shown on screen.
- **Order.** Topics are sequenced so no two adjacent episodes share a sub-niche.

## The 40 topics

### 01 · How to Sabotage · DECLASSIFIED
- **Anchor:** US Office of Strategic Services (OSS), *Simple Sabotage Field Manual*, dated 17 Jan 1944. The CIA released it in 2008.
- **Source:** CIA-hosted PDF: https://www.cia.gov/static/5c875f3ec660e092cf893f60b4a288df/SimpleSabotage.pdf (text also at Project Gutenberg #26184)
- **Finding:** The section "General Interference with Organizations and Production → Organizations and Conferences" tells citizen-saboteurs to:
  - "Insist on doing everything through 'channels'"
  - "Make 'speeches.' Talk as frequently as possible and at great length"
  - "refer all matters to committees… never less than five"
  - "Haggle over precise wordings"
  - "Advocate 'caution.'"
- **Status:** ROBUST (primary document). The step from organisations to personal habits is an analogy, so the script should frame it as one ("look familiar?").
- **Lesson:** Many of the habits that stall your goals are the ones saboteurs were trained to use; they just feel responsible.
- **Hook:** "In 1944, American spies wrote a manual for wrecking progress. You follow it."
- **Close:** "Which page are you following?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A filing cabinet with drawers labelled CHANNELS, COMMITTEE and LATER.
  - A ring of androids around a table, with HUD "DECISION: PENDING".
  - A red stamp reading "CAUTION" landing on a document while a clock spins.

### 02 · One Word Rewrites · MEMORY
- **Anchor:** Elizabeth Loftus & John Palmer, University of Washington, 1974.
- **Source:** *J. Verbal Learning & Verbal Behavior* 13(5):585–589. Author-hosted PDF: https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/faculty.sites.uci.edu/dist/c/571/files/2024/08/LoftusPalmer_ReconstructionAuto_JVLVB1974.pdf
- **Finding:** Students watched films of car crashes.
  - Speed estimates depended on the verb in the question: "smashed" gave a mean of 40.8 mph, "contacted" gave 31.8 mph.
  - A week later, asked whether they had seen broken glass (there was none), 32% of the "smashed" group said yes, against 14% of the "hit" group.
- **Status:** ROBUST. The misinformation effect has been replicated many times. Note that this is a lab study with film clips.
- **Lesson:** The words you use to retell a moment change what you later remember seeing.
- **Hook:** "There was no broken glass. A third of them saw it anyway." The "third" is the "smashed" group; say so in the anchor.
- **Close:** "Which word are you retelling it with?"
- **Visuals:**
  - Two cubes colliding on a grid, with HUD "GLASS: NONE".
  - Red shards forming inside an open filing-cabinet drawer.
  - A speedometer needle that jumps when the red word "SMASHED" appears.

### 03 · Suffering in Advance · PHILOSOPHY
- **Anchor:** Seneca, *Epistulae Morales* (Moral Letters to Lucilius), Letter 13, §4, c. AD 65.
- **Source:**
  - Latin: "Plura sunt, Lucili, quae nos terrent quam quae premunt, et saepius opinione quam re laboramus." (https://www.yorku.ca/pswarney/Texts/seneca/epistula/epistula-2-13-21.htm)
  - Gummere's Loeb translation (1917): "…we suffer more often in imagination than in reality." (https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_13)
- **Finding:** Seneca argues that more things frighten us than actually crush us, and that much distress comes from opinion and anticipation rather than events. He advises checking whether a feared harm is actually certain.
- **Status:** ROBUST (primary text). Literally, *opinione* means "opinion" or "supposition". "Imagination" is Gummere's rendering, so credit the translator if the line is shown on screen.
- **Lesson:** Much of what you suffer is rehearsal of events that never arrive.
- **Hook:** "Two thousand years ago, a Roman named the fear keeping you up."
- **Close:** "What are you suffering in advance?"
- **Visuals:**
  - An android in bed with red scenes projected on the ceiling.
  - A ghost hologram rehearsing a disaster beside the sleeping body.
  - An hourglass whose sand never reaches the bottom.

### 04 · Too Busy to See · CLASSIC · ✅ made as EP003 (trial, faster pacing)
- **Anchor:** John Darley & C. Daniel Batson, Princeton Theological Seminary, 1973 ("From Jerusalem to Jericho").
- **Source:** *JPSP* 27(1):100–108. Summary from Stanford SPARQ: https://sparq.stanford.edu/solutions/take-time-be-good-samaritan
- **Finding:** Seminarians walked to another building to give a talk, some of them on the Good Samaritan, and passed a slumped, groaning man in an alley.
  - The share who offered help fell with time pressure: 63% (low hurry), 45% (medium) and 10% (high hurry).
  - The topic of the talk made no significant difference.
- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED. The sample was small (40 analysed; one source says 67 were recruited). Use "in this study".
- **Lesson:** Hurry, more than character, decides who and what you notice.
- **Hook:** "They were rushing to preach about kindness. They stepped right past a man."
- **Close:** "Who did you walk past today?"
- **Visuals:**
  - An android walking fast past a slumped figure in a doorway, with HUD "LATE: YES".
  - Clock hands spinning above its head.
  - A red "10%" over an hourglass.

### 05 · Sleep On It · NEURO (sleep)
- **Anchor:** Ullrich Wagner, Steffen Gais, Hilde Haider, Rolf Verleger & Jan Born, University of Lübeck, 2004.
- **Source:** *Nature* 427:352–355, doi:10.1038/nature02223. PDF: https://pages.ucsd.edu/~achiba/SleepInsightWagnerNature04.pdf
- **Finding:** Participants practised a number task that had a hidden shortcut, then had 8 hours of night sleep, night wake or day wake. Afterwards, 13 of 22 in the sleep group (59.1%) discovered the shortcut, against 22.7% in each wake group.
- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED. One lab study, though it fits a larger literature on sleep and memory restructuring. Say "more than doubled", not "sleep guarantees insight".
- **Lesson:** Your sleeping brain reorganises what you learned and can hand you the shortcut you missed.
- **Hook:** "Sleep more than doubled the odds of seeing a hidden shortcut."
- **Close:** "What problem are you taking to bed?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A bed under a grid of numbers that rearranges itself overnight.
  - A light bulb switching on in a dark room.
  - A wire brain with a red path straightening.

### 06 · The Tiny Sign · SOCIAL
- **Anchor:** Jonathan Freedman & Scott Fraser, Stanford, homeowners in Palo Alto, 1966.
- **Source:** *JPSP* 4(2):195–202, doi:10.1037/h0023552. PDF: https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Influence_Compliance/Freedman_Fraser_Foot-in-the-door.pdf
- **Finding:** Homeowners were asked to put a large, badly lettered "Drive Carefully" sign in their front yard.
  - Asked cold, 16.7% agreed (n = 24).
  - Among those who two weeks earlier had agreed to display a small "Be a safe driver" sign, 76.0% agreed.
- **Status:** ROBUST as a phenomenon (foot-in-the-door). Later meta-analyses find smaller average effects than this study, so don't promise "4×" in general.
- **Lesson:** Each small yes quietly edits who you think you are, and the bigger yes follows.
- **Hook:** "A tiny sign got people to put a huge one on their lawn."
- **Close:** "Which small yes are you building on?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A tiny card on a door, then a giant billboard on the same house.
  - A stack of red tick marks growing beside the android.
  - A HUD reading "SELF-IMAGE: UPDATED".

### 07 · Darwin's Golden Rule · HISTORY
- **Anchor:** Charles Darwin, *Autobiography*, written 1876. First published 1887 in *Life and Letters*; unexpurgated edition by Barlow, 1958.
- **Source:** Darwin Online: https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1958_Autobiography_F1497/1958_Autobiography_F1497.html (excerpt also at https://bertie.ccsu.edu/darwinevol/DarwinAutoBiography.html)
- **Finding:** Darwin wrote: "I had, also, during many years, followed a golden rule, namely, that whenever a published fact, a new observation or thought came across me, which was opposed to my general results, to make a memorandum of it without fail and at once; for I had found by experience that such facts and thoughts were far more apt to escape from the memory than favourable ones."
- **Status:** ROBUST (primary text). This is Darwin's self-report, and it is consistent with later confirmation-bias research.
- **Lesson:** Your memory quietly drops the evidence against you; writing it down at once is how you keep it.
- **Hook:** "Darwin had one rule for the facts that proved him wrong."
- **Close:** "What evidence did you let slip today?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A filing cabinet in which red-tabbed cards slide out and vanish.
  - An android writing on a card under a light bulb.
  - A scale tipping, labelled "FOR" and "AGAINST".

### 08 · The Rigged Wheel · DECISION
- **Anchor:** Amos Tversky & Daniel Kahneman, 1974.
- **Source:** "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science* 185(4157):1124–1131, doi:10.1126/science.185.4157.1124
- **Finding:** A wheel numbered 0–100 was rigged to stop on 10 or 65. Subjects then estimated the percentage of African countries in the UN. Median estimates were 25% after seeing 10 and 45% after seeing 65.
- **Status:** ROBUST. Anchoring was among the strongest effects in Many Labs 1 (Klein et al. 2014, *Social Psychology* 45:142–152), using a related anchoring task rather than the wheel itself.
- **Lesson:** The first number you see bends every number after it, even one you know is random.
- **Hook:** "A rigged wheel of fortune changed what people believed about the world."
- **Close:** "What number did you hear first?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A spinning wheel with a red pointer locking on "65".
  - Price-tag cubes resizing toward the anchor.
  - A HUD reading "ANCHOR: 65 / ESTIMATE: 45".

### 09 · Sixty-Six Days · HABIT
- **Anchor:** Phillippa Lally, Cornelia van Jaarsveld, Henry Potts & Jane Wardle, University College London, 2010 (published online 2009).
- **Source:** *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6):998–1009, doi:10.1002/ejsp.674. UCL explainer: https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/bsh/2012/06/29/busting-the-21-days-habit-formation-myth/
- **Finding:** 96 volunteers repeated one new daily behaviour in a consistent context for 12 weeks, with automaticity tracked as a curve.
  - Among the 39 participants with good model fit, the median time to 95% of their plateau was 66 days, ranging from 18 to 254.
  - The top of the range is extrapolated, because the study ran only 84 days.
- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED (small sample, model-based). The popular "21 days" figure is unsupported. Say "a median of about 66 days, with a huge range".
- **Lesson:** A habit doesn't switch on at day 21; it curves up slowly, at your own pace.
- **Hook:** "Twenty-one days is a myth. The real number is stranger."
- **Close:** "What day are you on?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A calendar grid filling with red squares.
  - A rising curve that flattens on a grid floor.
  - An android walking the same path until it leaves a groove.

### 10 · The Psychic Files · DECLASSIFIED
- **Anchor:** American Institutes for Research (Mumford, Rose & Goslin), "An Evaluation of Remote Viewing: Research and Applications," 29 Sep 1995. This evaluated the US government's remote-viewing ("Star Gate") program; the document was approved for release in 2002.
- **Source:** CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180006-4: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/print/304505 (PDF mirror: https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/stargate/pdfs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180006-4.pdf)
- **Finding:** The executive summary says end users found the reports "inconsistent, inaccurate with regard to specifics" and needing "substantial subjective interpretation". It states that "In no case had the information provided ever been used to guide intelligence operations", and concludes that continued use is "not warranted".
- **Status:** ROBUST (primary document). The report also notes a "statistically significant" lab effect whose cause was unclear. Do not present that as proof of psychic ability. **Scheduling:** keep this far from the reference channel's Gateway episode, and make the lesson about interpretation, not about leaving the body.
- **Lesson:** Vague signals feel like hits because your mind does the matching.
- **Hook:** "For over twenty years, the US government funded psychic spying."
- **Close:** "Which vague sign are you reading into?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A wire sphere with a blurred red blob inside.
  - A stack of documents stamped "ACTIONABLE: NO".
  - An android tracing shapes into static on a grid.

### 11 · The Door Swap · ATTENTION
- **Anchor:** Daniel Simons & Daniel Levin, Cornell campus, 1998.
- **Source:** *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 5(4):644–649, doi:10.3758/BF03208840. https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03208840
- **Finding:** An experimenter asked a pedestrian for directions. Two men carried a door between them, and a different experimenter took his place.
  - Experiment 1: only 7 of 15 pedestrians noticed the switch.
  - Experiment 2: when the experimenters were dressed as construction workers, 4 of 12 students noticed.
- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED (small samples). Change blindness in general is robust. **Distinct from the excluded doorway effect:** the door here is just an occluder. Don't mention room boundaries or forgetting.
- **Lesson:** You don't record the world; you record the gist and fill in the rest.
- **Hook:** "Mid-conversation, the stranger became someone else. Half of them kept talking."
- **Close:** "Who have you stopped actually looking at?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A wooden door sliding between two androids, and a different android standing there after it passes.
  - A HUD reading "SUBJECT: CHANGED / DETECTED: NO".
  - A map card in hand.

### 12 · Rising Unwillingly · PHILOSOPHY
- **Anchor:** Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.1, c. AD 170–180.
- **Source:** George Long translation: https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.5.five.html (also https://lexundria.com/m_aur_med/5.1/lg)
- **Finding:** "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present—I am rising to the work of a human being. Why then am I dissatisfied if I am going to do the things for which I exist and for which I was brought into the world?" He goes on to ask whether he was made to lie in the bedclothes and keep warm.
- **Status:** ROBUST (primary text, a private notebook to himself).
- **Lesson:** Even an emperor argued with his bed each morning; what got him up was a reason, not a feeling.
- **Hook:** "A Roman emperor left himself notes about getting out of bed."
- **Close:** "What are you rising for tomorrow?"
- **Visuals:**
  - An android sitting on the edge of a bed in grey dawn light.
  - A clock at 5:00 with a red ring.
  - A door with light under it, and a red word "WORK".

### 13 · Pigeon Rituals · CLASSIC
- **Anchor:** B. F. Skinner, Harvard, 1948.
- **Source:** "'Superstition' in the pigeon," *J. Experimental Psychology* 38:168–172: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Skinner/Pigeon/
- **Finding:** Hungry pigeons got food every 15 seconds no matter what they did. In 6 of 8 birds, distinct rituals developed, such as turning counter-clockwise or thrusting the head into a corner, as if the action produced the food.
- **Status:** ROBUST observation. Staddon & Simmelhag (1971) reinterpreted part of it as food-anticipation behaviour, so say "Skinner called it superstition."
- **Lesson:** Your brain ties whatever you happened to be doing to whatever good came next, and turns it into a ritual.
- **Hook:** "Skinner fed pigeons at random. They invented rituals to make it happen."
- **Close:** "Which of your rituals actually works?"
- **Visuals:**
  - An android turning in circles inside a wire box.
  - A clock ticking 15 s, then a light bulb flash.
  - A red loop ring tracing the repeated path.

### 14 · Name It · NEURO (emotion)
- **Anchor:** Matthew Lieberman, Naomi Eisenberger, Molly Crockett and colleagues, UCLA, 2007.
- **Source:** *Psychological Science* 18(5):421–428, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x. PubMed 17576282: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/
- **Finding:** In fMRI, labelling emotional faces with an emotion word reduced amygdala response compared with other ways of processing them. It also increased right ventrolateral prefrontal activity, and the two were inversely related.
- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED. This is a single fMRI study and the brain interpretation is correlational. Say "was linked to less alarm activity", not "shuts off fear".
- **Lesson:** Putting a precise word on a feeling recruits your thinking brain and can turn down the alarm.
- **Hook:** "One word can turn down the alarm inside your head."
- **Close:** "What's the exact word for it?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A wire brain whose red core dims as a word appears.
  - The android whispering, with HUD "LABEL: FEAR".
  - A siren light fading to grey.

### 15 · The Moving Dot · SOCIAL
- **Anchor:** Muzafer Sherif, Columbia University, 1935 (autokinetic studies).
- **Source:** "A study of some social factors in perception," *Archives of Psychology* 27(187). Full text at the Mead Project: https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Sherif/Sherif_1935a/Sherif_1935a_2.html
- **Finding:** A fixed pinpoint of light in a dark room appears to move. When people called out estimates in groups, their numbers converged on a shared group norm, and individuals later tested alone kept using it.
- **Status:** ROBUST classic. Don't add numbers; the procedure itself is the story.
- **Lesson:** When reality is ambiguous, you borrow other people's numbers and keep them after they leave.
- **Hook:** "The light never moved. The whole room agreed on how far."
- **Close:** "Whose number are you still using?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A single red dot in a black void.
  - Three androids with HUD numbers drifting toward one value.
  - Ghost figures leaving while the number stays.

### 16 · Franklin's Chart · HISTORY
- **Anchor:** Benjamin Franklin, *Autobiography*, Part Two, written 1784, describing his plan of c. 1730.
- **Source:** https://www.ushistory.org/franklin/autobiography/page38.htm (Fordham excerpt: https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/franklin-virtue.asp)
- **Finding:** Franklin listed 13 virtues and made a little book with one page per virtue. Each page was ruled into seven day-columns crossed by 13 red lines, and he marked each fault with a black spot. He gave "strict attention" to one virtue per week, so a full course took 13 weeks.
- **Status:** ROBUST (primary self-account). He admits he never reached his ideal; use that honestly.
- **Lesson:** You can't fix thirteen things at once; one tracked thing a week compounds.
- **Hook:** "Benjamin Franklin kept a weekly scorecard of his own failures."
- **Close:** "Which one gets your week?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A grid chart with red lines and black dots filling in.
  - Calendar weeks flipping.
  - An android at a desk with a small book under a lamp.

### 17 · The Thesis Clock · DECISION
- **Anchor:** Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin & Michael Ross, 1994. The term "planning fallacy" comes from Kahneman & Tversky, 1979.
- **Source:** "Exploring the 'planning fallacy'," *JPSP* 67(3):366–381, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366. Summary: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy
- **Finding:** 37 students predicted when they would finish their honours thesis.
  - Mean prediction: 33.9 days. Mean actual: 55.5 days.
  - Even their worst-case estimate (48.6 days on average) was met by fewer than half of them.
- **Status:** ROBUST (widely replicated).
- **Lesson:** You plan from the best-case story in your head, not from how long it actually took you last time.
- **Hook:** "Even their worst-case guess was too optimistic."
- **Close:** "How long did it take last time?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A calendar with red X's running past a circled deadline.
  - Two clocks drifting apart, labelled PLAN and REAL.
  - An hourglass refilling.

### 18 · When and Where · HABIT
- **Anchor:** Peter Gollwitzer & Veronika Brandstätter, 1997 (the Christmas Eve report study).
- **Source:** *JPSP* 73(1):186–199, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.73.1.186. Summary in Gollwitzer 1999, *American Psychologist* 54(7):493–503: https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/99_Gollwitzer_Implementation_Intentions.pdf
- **Finding:** Students had to write a report on Christmas Eve within 48 hours. Of those who wrote down exactly when and where they would do it, "three fourths" sent it in on time, against "one third" of controls.
- **Status:** ROBUST but smaller than first thought. The 2006 meta-analysis (94 tests) found d = .65. A newer 642-test synthesis (Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer) reports a smaller effect after bias correction. Say "far more likely in this study". **Keep distinct from EP001:** frame this as handing the start to a cue, not as closing a loop.
- **Lesson:** A goal says what; an if-then plan tells your brain when and where, and lets the situation start it for you.
- **Hook:** "One sentence on paper, and people became twice as likely to follow through."
- **Close:** "When, exactly? And where?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A calendar date with a red ring and a clock pinned to it.
  - A door that opens by itself when the clock hits the time.
  - A HUD reading "IF 7:00 → THEN WRITE".

### 19 · The More-Data Trap · DECLASSIFIED
- **Anchor:** Richards J. Heuer Jr., *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, CIA Center for the Study of Intelligence, 1999. It draws on internal CIA articles from 1978–86; see Chapter 5, "Do You Really Need More Information?"
- **Source:** https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/books-monographs/psychology-of-intelligence-analysis-2/ (Ch. 5 text: https://all.net/journal/deception/ciabook2/www.cia.gov/csi/books/19104/art8.html)
- **Finding:** Eight experienced horserace handicappers predicted 40 past races using their top 5, 10, 20 and then 40 variables. "Average accuracy of predictions remained the same," yet "all… expressed steadily increasing confidence" as information grew. With 5 items, their confidence was well calibrated.
- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED. This is a small study reported second-hand in a CIA training text, so frame it as "an experiment the CIA uses to train analysts".
- **Lesson:** More information often raises your confidence, not your accuracy.
- **Hook:** "A CIA textbook warns: more data made experts surer, not better."
- **Close:** "Do you need more, or to decide?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A growing stack of files with a red confidence bar rising while a grey accuracy bar stays flat.
  - An android buried in folders.
  - A HUD reading "CONFIDENCE: ↑ / ACCURACY: —".

### 20 · Vivid Isn't True · MEMORY
- **Anchor:** Jennifer Talarico & David Rubin, Duke University, from 12 Sep 2001.
- **Source:** "Confidence, not consistency, characterizes flashbulb memories," *Psychological Science* 14(5):455–461, doi:10.1111/1467-9280.02453. Duke repository: https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/057d8b9b-ab52-4e5f-94ff-f42bb22ca1b1
- **Finding:** 54 students recorded how they heard about 9/11, plus one everyday memory, and were retested 1, 6 or 32 weeks later. Both memories became less consistent at similar rates. Only for 9/11 did vividness and confidence stay high.
- **Status:** ROBUST. It fits Neisser & Harsch's Challenger study. Treat the topic respectfully: no imagery of the attacks.
- **Lesson:** Feeling certain about a memory is not evidence that it's accurate.
- **Hook:** "Your most vivid memory may be one of your least accurate."
- **Close:** "Which memory are you most sure of?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A calendar page that slowly rewrites itself inside a filing drawer.
  - A light bulb labelled CONFIDENCE staying bright while the document beside it fades.
  - A red ring around a date.

### 21 · Not the Thing · PHILOSOPHY
- **Anchor:** Epictetus, *Enchiridion* (Handbook) §5, compiled by Arrian, c. AD 125.
- **Source:** George Long translation: https://cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0557/tlg002/perseus-eng3/5
- **Finding:** "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things." Death, for example, is not terrible, or it would have seemed so to Socrates; the opinion that it is terrible is the terrible thing. So when troubled, look to your own judgements.
- **Status:** ROBUST (primary text). Space it well apart from #03 (Seneca).
- **Lesson:** The event and your verdict on it arrive together, so they feel like one thing; they're not.
- **Hook:** "It isn't what happened that's hurting you right now."
- **Close:** "What verdict did you stamp on it?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A plain grey cube, then a red label slapping onto it.
  - The android peeling the label off.
  - Split white/black frames: EVENT and VERDICT.

### 22 · The Empty Because · CLASSIC
- **Anchor:** Ellen Langer, Arthur Blank & Benzion Chanowitz, City University of New York (CUNY) Graduate Center, 1978. Someone asked to cut in line at a photocopier.
- **Source:** *JPSP* 36(6):635–642, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.36.6.635. Scan: https://jamesclear.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/copy-machine-study-ellen-langer.pdf
- **Finding:** For a small request (5 pages), compliance was:
  - 60% with no reason given;
  - 94% with a real reason;
  - 93% with an empty reason ("because I have to make copies").
- **Status:** **CONTESTED.** In the same paper, the empty reason did not work for a larger (20-page) request, and Folkes (1985) failed to replicate the effect. The script must say "in one 1978 study, for a small favour" and should add a turn such as "and it only worked when the stakes were tiny", which supports the lesson.
- **Lesson:** On autopilot, your mind checks that a reason exists, not whether it's any good.
- **Hook:** "One empty word got strangers to let someone cut the line."
- **Close:** "Whose 'because' are you obeying?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A queue of androids at a copier, one stepping ahead.
  - A red "BECAUSE" tag stamping each request.
  - A HUD reading "REASON: EMPTY / COMPLIANCE: YES".

### 23 · The Promise Signal · NEURO (reward)
- **Anchor:** Wolfram Schultz, Peter Dayan & P. Read Montague, 1997, synthesising Schultz's monkey recordings.
- **Source:** "A neural substrate of prediction and reward," *Science* 275(5306):1593–1599, doi:10.1126/science.275.5306.1593. PubMed 9054347: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9054347/
- **Finding:**
  - Dopamine neurons fire to an unexpected reward.
  - Once a cue reliably predicts the reward, they respond to the cue and little to the predicted reward itself.
  - If the expected reward fails to arrive, activity dips below baseline: a "reward prediction error" signal.
- **Status:** ROBUST (foundational). Don't call dopamine the "pleasure chemical", and when moving from monkeys and juice to phones, signal that it's an analogy.
- **Lesson:** Your reward system learns to fire at the promise and sags when the promise breaks, which is why the buzz pulls harder than the message.
- **Hook:** "Your brain's reward signal learns to fire before the reward arrives."
- **Close:** "What cue is pulling at you now?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A phone buzzing with a red pulse racing to a wire brain.
  - A light-bulb cue followed by a cup, with the spike moving from cup to bulb.
  - A flatline dip when the cup is empty.

### 24 · Looking Up · SOCIAL
- **Anchor:** Stanley Milgram, Leonard Bickman & Lawrence Berkowitz, a New York City sidewalk, 1968–69.

- **Finding:** Crowds of 1 to 15 confederates stood staring up at a sixth-floor window for 60 s. As the crowd grew, a larger share of passersby looked up and stopped. Secondary summaries report about 4% stopping for one starer and about 40% for fifteen.

- **Lesson:** Your attention is contagious: you look where others look before you ask why.
- **Hook:** "Fifteen strangers stared at an empty window. The street stopped with them."

### 26 · Choice Blindness · DECISION
- **Anchor:** Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström & Andreas Olsson, Lund University, 2005.
- **Source:** "Failure to detect mismatches between intention and outcome in a simple decision task," *Science* 310(5745):116–119, doi:10.1126/science.1111709. https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/220623
- **Finding:** Participants chose the more attractive of two faces. Through sleight of hand, they were sometimes handed the face they had *not* chosen. Most switches went unnoticed, and people gave reasons for the choice they never made.
- **Status:** ROBUST within the paradigm; the Lund group extended it to taste and political attitudes. Reported detection rates vary by measure (roughly 10–20%), so say "most didn't notice".
- **Lesson:** You often make the choice first and write the reason afterwards.

### 27 · Fresh Start · HABIT

- **Finding:** Three kinds of goal behaviour all rose after "temporal landmarks": Google searches for "diet", university gym visits, and goal commitments. The landmarks were the start of a week, month, year or semester, birthdays (except 21sts) and holidays.

### 28 · The NASA Nap · GOVERNMENT
- **Anchor:** Mark Rosekind, R. Curtis Graeber, David Dinges and colleagues, NASA Ames Research Center with the FAA, 1994.
- **Source:** "Crew Factors in Flight Operations IX: Effects of Planned Cockpit Rest on Crew Performance and Alertness in Long-Haul Operations," NASA TM-108839 (DOT/FAA/92/24). https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19950006379
- **Finding:** 21 long-haul pilots were randomly assigned to a Rest or No-Rest group.
  - The Rest group got a planned 40-minute rest opportunity in cruise; pilots fell asleep in about 5.6 min and slept about 26 min.

- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED (n = 21). Do **not** use the widely quoted "34% performance / 54% alertness" figures, which were not found in the report.
- **Lesson:** A short planned rest is a performance tool, not a weakness.
- **Hook:** "NASA let pilots nap mid-flight, on purpose, and measured what happened."
- **Close:** "When did you last rest on purpose?"
- **Visuals:**

### 29 · The Forgetting Curve · MEMORY
- **Anchor:** Hermann Ebbinghaus, *Über das Gedächtnis* (Memory), Leipzig, 1885. He was his own only subject, learning lists of nonsense syllables. Replicated by Jaap Murre & Joeri Dros, 2015.
- **Source:**
  - 1913 English translation: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Ebbinghaus/index.htm
  - Murre & Dros, *PLOS ONE* 10(7):e0120644, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0120644
- **Finding:** Ebbinghaus measured how much effort relearning saved. Forgetting is steep at first and then levels off. In the 2015 replication, one subject spent 70 hours learning and relearning lists and produced a very similar curve, with a possible upward bump at 24 h.
- **Status:** ROBUST (shape of the curve). Exact percentages depend on the material, so avoid the popular "you forget 70% in a day".
- **Lesson:** Most forgetting happens fast, so one well-timed review beats rereading.
- **Hook:** "A man memorised nonsense for years, just to measure forgetting."
- **Close:** "What did you learn this week — and lose?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A red curve plunging, then flattening, on a grid.
  - Filing drawers emptying in sequence.
  - A single review card that pushes the curve back up.

### 30 · The Quiet Room · PHILOSOPHY + experiment
- **Anchor:** Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* fragment 139 ("Diversion", Brunschvicg numbering; published 1670), tested by Timothy Wilson and colleagues, University of Virginia & Harvard, 2014.
- **Source:**
  - Trotter translation: https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/mod/1660pascal-pensees.asp
  - Wilson et al., "Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind," *Science* 345(6192):75–77, doi:10.1126/science.1250830
- **Finding:**
  - Pascal: "all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber."
  - Wilson et al.: across 11 studies, people left alone 6–15 minutes with nothing to do generally found it unpleasant. In one study, 67% of men and 25% of women gave themselves at least one mild electric shock rather than just think.
- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED. Critics (Fox et al. 2014) note the shock was mild and many still preferred thinking, so say "some chose a shock". Fragment numbers differ by edition (Lafuma 136).
- **Lesson:** Your restlessness isn't about the room; it's about being alone with your own head.
- **Hook:** "Left alone with their thoughts, some people chose an electric shock."
- **Close:** "Could you sit with it for fifteen minutes?"
- **Visuals:**

  - An android seated, its hand drifting toward the button.
  - An hourglass marked 15.

### 31 · The One-Dollar Lie · CLASSIC
- **Anchor:** Leon Festinger & James Carlsmith, Stanford, 1959.
- **Source:** "Cognitive consequences of forced compliance," *J. Abnormal and Social Psychology* 58(2):203–210, doi:10.1037/h0041593. https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Festinger/
- **Finding:** After an hour of tedious peg-turning, students were paid $1 or $20 to tell the next participant it was fun. Later enjoyment ratings, on a scale of −5 to +5 with 20 per group:
  - $1 group: +1.35
  - $20 group: −0.05
  - Controls: −0.45
- **Status:** **CONTESTED.** A 2024 registered replication of the related induced-compliance paradigm across 39 labs (N = 4,898; Vaidis et al., *AMPPS* 7(1), doi:10.1177/25152459231213375) found no high- vs low-choice difference. The script must say "in the original 1959 study", and add one line such as "newer, bigger studies question how strong this is."
- **Lesson:** When an action has no good outside reason, your mind may quietly rewrite your beliefs to fit it.
- **Hook:** "Pay someone a dollar to lie, and they start believing it."
- **Close:** "What did you talk yourself into?"
- **Visuals:**
  - A pegboard grid of turning cubes.
  - Two coins on a balance, $1 and $20.
  - An android putting on a smiling mask that fuses to its face.

### 32 · Edison's Steel Ball · NEURO (sleep onset)
- **Anchor:** Célia Lacaux, Thomas Andrillon, Delphine Oudiette and colleagues, Paris Brain Institute, 2021.
- **Source:** "Sleep onset is a creative sweet spot," *Science Advances* 7(50):eabj5866, doi:10.1126/sciadv.abj5866. PMC8654287: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8654287/
- **Finding:** 103 participants worked on math problems with a hidden rule, then took a 20-min rest holding an object, Edison-style. Those who spent at least 15 s in N1, the first light stage of sleep, found the hidden rule 83% of the time, against 30% for those who stayed awake. The benefit vanished if they went into deeper sleep.
- **Status:** SOLID, LIMITED (single study). The Edison steel-ball habit is a legend, so call it "the story goes". Space it apart from #05 and #25.
- **Lesson:** There's a creative window at the edge of sleep, but only if you don't fall through it.
- **Hook:** "Edison napped holding steel balls. In 2021, scientists finally tested why."
- **Close:** "What shows up right before you sleep?"
- **Visuals:**

### 33 · Watching Eyes · SOCIAL

### 34 · Hands Remember · HISTORY
- **Anchor:** Henry Molaison ("H.M."), after surgery in 1953 by William Scoville, studied by Brenda Milner (McGill / Montreal Neurological Institute).

- **Finding:** H.M. could no longer form new conscious memories. Asked to trace a star while seeing only its mirror image, he improved steadily across three days of practice while having no memory of ever doing the task.

### 35 · The Right-Hand Pick · DECISION
- **Anchor:** Richard Nisbett & Timothy Wilson, University of Michigan, 1977 (shoppers judging stockings and nightgowns).

- **Finding:** Shoppers compared four identical pairs of stockings and favoured the right-most pair. When asked why, they cited quality, and none mentioned position. Most dismissed position as a factor when it was suggested to them.
- **Status:** ROBUST as a classic demonstration of the limits of introspection. A 2025 critique argues it is often over-read. Don't quote a ratio (the "4-to-1" figure is unconfirmed), and claim only that "they couldn't see the real reason".
- **Lesson:** The reasons you give for your choices can be confident guesses about a process you can't see.
- **Hook:** "Four identical items. People explained exactly why theirs was better."

### 39 · The Worry Appointment · HABIT

### 40 · Remember, Rewrite · NEURO (memory)
- **Anchor:** Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe & Joseph LeDoux, New York University, 2000.

- **Finding:** In rats, reactivating a consolidated fear memory and then blocking protein synthesis in the amygdala produced amnesia for that memory. Memories that were not reactivated were unaffected. Recalled memories become temporarily unstable and must be stored again.
- **Status:** ROBUST in animals. Applications in humans (e.g. drug-assisted therapy) are mixed, so don't say you can "delete" memories.
- **Lesson:** Each time you recall something, it's briefly open for editing, so how you revisit it matters.
- **Hook:** "Every time you remember something, you might be rewriting it."
- **Close:** "What will you change next time?"
- **Visuals:**

  - A wire brain with an amygdala core flickering.
  - A red ring closing around the drawer.

## Rejected / not usable (failed verification or too unreliable)

1. **Viktor Frankl: "Between stimulus and response there is a space…".** Not found in Frankl's works; the Viktor Frankl Institute lists it as an "alleged quote" (quoteinvestigator.com/2018/02/18/response).
2. **"Military method: fall asleep in 2 minutes, 96% success" (Bud Winter, *Relax and Win*, 1981).** No controlled study; the figure traces to the book and blogs.
3. **Ego depletion (Baumeister 1998, the "radish" study).** The 23-lab registered replication (Hagger et al. 2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11:546–573) found d = 0.04, with a confidence interval that includes zero.
4. **"Neurons that fire together wire together" as a Hebb quote.** It's a 1992 paraphrase (Shatz / Löwel), not Hebb's 1949 wording. Usable only as a paraphrase.
5. **NASA nap "improves performance 34% and alertness 54%."** Not found in TM-108839; the topic is kept (#28) without these numbers.
6. **Milgram crowd study "42% / 86% looked up."** Couldn't confirm the looking-up figures; #24 uses the stopping trend only.
7. **Nisbett & Wilson "right-most chosen four to one."** The ratio was not confirmable from available text; #35 stays qualitative.
8. **Langer 1975 "illusion of control" lottery ($8.67 vs $1.96).** The prices were seen only in a secondary review, and sources disagree on which experiment they come from. Dropped.
9. **"Hungry judges" (Danziger et al. 2011, PNAS).** Published critiques (e.g. Weinshall-Margel & Shapard 2011, PNAS) argue that case ordering explains the pattern. Too contested for an anchor.
10. **Rosenhan 1973, "On being sane in insane places."** The study's reliability is seriously questioned (Cahalan, *The Great Pretender*, 2019). Not usable as a factual anchor.
